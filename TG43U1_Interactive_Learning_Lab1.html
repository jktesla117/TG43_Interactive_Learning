Understanding the TG-43 brachytherapy dose formalism, term by term and in three dimensions

Five terms multiply together to turn a source's air-kerma strength into a dose rate at any point in tissue. This page lets you rotate the geometry, drag the source, and watch each term move — instead of just reading the equation.

§1 — Overview

Why brachytherapy needs its own formalism

In external-beam radiotherapy the beam is far from the patient, so dose falls off gently and fairly uniformly through the treated volume. In brachytherapy the source sits inside or against the tissue being treated, often only millimeters from the region of interest. Dose can change by a factor of two over a single centimeter, and the source itself is not a mathematical point — it is a small cylinder with its own internal absorption and encapsulation.

The original 1995 TG-43 report, and the 2004 update this page is based on, solved that problem by splitting the dose calculation into separate, independently-measurable pieces: how fast dose falls off with distance for an idealized source shape, how the real source's finite size and self-absorption modify that fall-off, and how the dose depends on angle relative to the source's long axis. Each piece can be measured or simulated once for a given source model and then reused in any patient geometry.

Scope note. TG-43 assumes a homogeneous, water-equivalent medium and a single source with no shielding or interseed attenuation from neighboring sources or applicators. It applies to the low- and intermediate-energy photon-emitting sources used in permanent seed implants and HDR/PDR afterloading.

§2 — The dose-rate equation

One equation, five terms

Click any term below — the equation, the definitions, and the calculators later on the page all use this same anatomy.

Ḋ(r,θ)  =  Sk · Λ · [GL(r,θ) / GL(r0,θ0)] · gL(r) · F(r,θ)
Sk
Air-kerma strength — the source's output, measured in air, independent of any patient. units: U = cGy·cm²·h⁻¹
Λ
Dose-rate constant — the dose rate the source actually produces at the reference point (1 cm, 90°) in water, per unit of air-kerma strength. Converts the source's "output rating" into an actual in-tissue dose rate. units: cGy·h⁻¹·U⁻¹
GL
Geometry function — the purely geometric fall-off of dose with distance, for an idealized line (or point) of the source's shape. Carries the inverse-square law and, for a line source, the fact that a finite rod looks different up close than a point does. Divided by its own value at the reference point so the ratio equals 1 there.
gL(r)
Radial dose function — everything the geometry function leaves out along the transverse direction (θ = 90°): photon attenuation through tissue and the source's own material, plus scatter build-up. Normalized to 1 at r₀ = 1 cm.
F(r,θ)
Anisotropy function — how dose at a given radius varies with angle away from 90°. Captures oblique filtration through the source's encapsulation, which reduces dose near the source's long axis (θ near 0° or 180°). Normalized to 1 at θ₀ = 90°.

§3 — Coordinate frame & geometry function

Where r and θ actually live

Every term in the equation is a function of just two numbers: r, the distance from the center of the source, and θ, the angle measured from the source's own long axis. The reference point used to normalize everything is fixed at r₀ = 1 cm, θ₀ = 90° — directly out to the side of the source, one centimeter away.

The geometry function itself comes in two flavors. Treating the source as a point gives the familiar inverse-square law, 1/r². Treating it as the line it actually is — a rod of active length L — accounts for the fact that close to the source, different parts of that rod are at noticeably different distances from your calculation point. That correction, β, is the angle the active length subtends as seen from wherever you're standing.

§4 — Radial dose function

What the geometry function doesn't explain

Strip out the inverse-square fall-off (that's what dividing by the geometry function does) and what's left is gL(r): pure tissue physics along the transverse line, θ = 90°. It is shaped by two competing effects — photons get absorbed and scattered out of the beam as they travel further (which lowers g), while scattered photons from all directions build back up around the detector (which raises it).

How those two effects trade off depends heavily on photon energy. Low-energy sources like ¹²⁵I and ¹⁰³Pd are dominated by photoelectric absorption, so g(r) drops off far more steeply with distance than a higher-energy source like ¹⁹²Ir.

§5 — Anisotropy function

Why the ends of the source are "colder"

A real source is encapsulated in a titanium or steel shell. Photons traveling nearly parallel to the source's long axis (θ near 0° or 180°) have to punch through much more of that shell — and, for seeds, more of the radioactive core's own self-absorption — than photons headed straight out the side. The result is a consistent dip in dose near the source's tips, deepest for low-energy sources whose photons are most easily absorbed.

The polar plot traces relative dose around the source at a fixed radius: a perfect circle would mean no anisotropy at all.
